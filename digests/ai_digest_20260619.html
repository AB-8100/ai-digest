📐 ML & Technical Fundamentals

Explainer: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) — Why It's Still the Default Enterprise Architecture

RAG remains the most-deployed AI architecture in enterprise in 2026, and understanding it properly separates AI PMs who can have credible technical conversations from those who can't. The core idea: rather than fine-tuning a model on proprietary data (expensive, requires retraining when data changes), you retrieve relevant documents at inference time and pass them as context to the model. The model then answers grounded in those documents rather than its training weights alone.

The practical architecture: a document ingestion pipeline chunks your corpus, embeds it into a vector database (Pinecone, Weaviate, pgvector), then at query time your application embeds the user's question, retrieves the top-k most semantically similar chunks, and stuffs them into the model's context window alongside the query. The model generates a response grounded in that retrieved context. For enterprise use cases — internal knowledge bases, contract review, regulatory compliance — this is almost always the right starting point over fine-tuning because: (1) data updates don't require retraining; (2) you can audit which documents drove the response; (3) you avoid catastrophic forgetting.

The current evolution is agentic RAG: rather than a single retrieval-then-generate step, the model iteratively decides whether to retrieve more information, which query to run, and when it has enough context. Google's Vector Search 2.0 is now generally available — a retrieval engine designed to streamline AI development and serve as the knowledge core for AI applications. NotebookLM's June update (agentic source discovery) is essentially a consumer-facing implementation of agentic RAG.

Failure modes to brief stakeholders on: (1) Chunking strategy matters enormously — wrong chunk size loses context; (2) retrieval recall is not the same as answer quality — a model can retrieve the right document and still misinterpret it; (3) hallucination is not eliminated by RAG, just grounded — the model can confabulate based on retrieved text. For a production-grade explainer, see Lewis et al. (2020), the original RAG paper on arXiv and the LlamaIndex documentation.

🔬 Technical Deep Dive

Speculative Decoding: The Technique Cutting Enterprise Inference Costs by 2–3×

Speculative decoding is the inference optimisation technique that has moved from research curiosity to production standard over the past 18 months — and it directly affects the cost and latency of every AI product deployed at scale. For AI PMs evaluating build vs. buy on inference infrastructure, understanding this is worth the 10 minutes it takes.

The problem it solves: the critical performance constraint in single-batch LLM inference is memory bandwidth, not compute capacity. Each token generation requires loading the full model weights from GPU memory, which leaves arithmetic units largely idle. This makes token generation slow and expensive regardless of how much compute you have. Speculative decoding is an inference-time optimisation that speeds up LLM token generation without reducing output quality. It works by pairing two models: a smaller, fast draft model that proposes several draft tokens ahead, and a larger model that verifies these proposed tokens in parallel and accepts those that match its own predictions. The key property: speculative decoding achieves 2–3× latency improvements while maintaining mathematically identical output quality — by exploiting the asymmetry between sequential generation and parallel verification, allowing a small draft model to propose multiple token candidates that a larger target model can verify simultaneously, sidestepping the memory bandwidth bottleneck without architectural changes or quality trade-offs.

The current frontier implementations go beyond the simple small-model-plus-large-model setup. EAGLE3 (the third iteration of the EAGLE speculative decoding method) uses the target model's own feature representations to train a lightweight draft head directly on the larger model, achieving acceptance rates above 80% — much higher than using a separate smaller model. Medusa takes a different approach: adding multiple decoding heads to the original model in parallel, each predicting tokens at different positions ahead. Both are now production-ready and available in vLLM and TensorRT-LLM.

Google introduced speculative decoding in a 2022 paper and has since deployed it in AI Overviews; the original paper demonstrated 2-3x improvements on translation and summarisation; in 2025 and 2026, the technique went from research experiment to production standard, now built into vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM, and most serious serving frameworks. For enterprise AI PMs: this is why inference costs have fallen faster than model capabilities alone would predict. When evaluating a vendor's cost-per-token claims, ask whether speculative decoding is enabled and what acceptance rates they're achieving on your specific task distribution — it's the single biggest lever available without model degradation.
